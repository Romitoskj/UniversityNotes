# Diffusion Models

### **1. The Core Problem: Modeling Distributions and The Partition Function**

The ultimate goal of a generative model is to learn an unknown data distribution $p_{\text{true}}(x)$ (e.g., the probability distribution of all images of a human face) so that we can sample from it to generate new, realistic data.

A common way to define any probability distribution is through an **energy-based formulation** (the Boltzmann distribution from physics): $p_\theta(x) = \frac{1}{Z_\theta} \exp(-E_\theta(x))$.

- **The Energy Function $E_\theta(x)$:** This is a parametric function (like a neural network) that assigns low energy to highly likely data (real images) and high energy to unlikely data (noise).
- **The Partition Function $Z_\theta$:** Because a probability distribution must integrate to 1, we must divide by $Z_\theta$, which is the integral of the numerator over the entire data space.

**The Bottleneck:** Computing $Z_\theta$ is computationally intractable because it requires integrating over all possible images. Because of this, we cannot simply optimize $p_\theta(x)$ directly, and we need clever mathematical tricks to bypass the partition function.

### **2. Score Matching (2005) & Langevin Dynamics**

In 2005, Hyvärinen proposed a brilliant workaround to the partition function: **Score Matching**.

- **The Score Function:** Instead of modeling the probability density directly, we can model the _gradient_ of the log-likelihood with respect to the data $x$: $\nabla_x \log p_\theta(x)$.
- **Why it works:** Because $\log p_\theta(x) = -E_\theta(x) - \log Z_\theta$, taking the derivative with respect to $x$ completely eliminates the constant $Z_\theta$. The score is exactly equal to the negative gradient of the energy function: $-\nabla_x E_\theta(x)$.
- **Langevin Dynamics (Sampling):** The score function creates a vector field of arrows pointing toward the direction of highest probability. To generate a new image, you can start from pure noise and iteratively follow these arrows (gradient ascent on the log probability). By intentionally adding a small amount of random noise at each step to explore the space, this iterative process is called Langevin dynamics.

### **3. Denoising Score Matching (2011)**

While original Score Matching was conceptually brilliant, it failed in practice because real high-dimensional data (like images) is extremely sparse. You cannot compute a numerical derivative if you only have isolated data points with massive empty spaces between them.

- **Densifying the Space:** Pascal Vincent solved this by deliberately corrupting the training images with Gaussian noise $\tilde{x} = x + \epsilon$. This smooths out the isolated data points into continuous "bumps", densifying the local neighborhood and allowing the network to compute gradients.
- **The Model's Output:** The network learns to predict a **denoising direction**—a vector pointing from the noisy image back to the clean image. Vincent proved that training a model to denoise these corrupted images (equivalent to a Denoising Autoencoder) is mathematically equivalent to matching the true score of the data distribution.

### **4. Energy-Based Models (2019)**

Energy-Based Models (EBMs) returned to training the energy function $E_\theta(x)$ directly using a method called **Contrastive Divergence**.

- **Contrastive Training:** To approximate the intractable gradient for training, EBMs evaluate the energy difference between "knowingly good" samples (real training data) and "knowingly bad" samples (fake data generated by the model itself on the fly).
- This pushes the energy of real data down and the energy of fake data up, preventing "mode collapse".

### **5. Noise Conditional Score Networks (NCSN)**

Building on Vincent's work, Song & Ermon (2019) introduced NCSNs. Instead of training the model on a single, fixed noise variance, they conditioned the score network on a **schedule of multiple noise levels** ($\sigma_i$).

- By training a single model (often a U-Net) to denoise across all variance levels simultaneously, they created a network capable of starting from pure, high-variance noise and iteratively stepping down the noise schedule until a pristine image emerges.

### **6. Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPMs)**

DDPMs (2020) are the foundation of modern generative AI (like Stable Diffusion). They define two explicit processes:

- **The Forward Process (Diffusion):** An iterative Markov chain that slowly destroys an image by adding small amounts of Gaussian noise $\beta_t$ over $T$ steps, until the image is indistinguishable from pure noise $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$. Thanks to Gaussian properties, we can bypass the step-by-step process and jump directly to any noisy timestep $t$ using the formula: $x_t = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\epsilon$.
- **The Backward Process (Generation):** A neural network (U-Net) is trained to reverse this process. Instead of predicting the clean image directly, the network $\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t)$ is trained to predict the exact **noise $\epsilon$ that was added** to the image at step $t$.
- **Sampling:** To generate an image, we sample pure noise and iteratively subtract the network's predicted noise using the derived posterior distribution, stepping backwards from $T$ to $0$.

### **7. Flow Matching (The Latest Evolution)**

Flow matching is introduced as a simpler, continuous, and highly effective alternative to standard diffusion models.

- **The Painter Analogy & "On-the-Fly" Datasets:** The generative process mimics how a human painter works. A painter does not draw a perfect final image pixel-by-pixel in one shot; they start with a blank canvas and iteratively add details like shadows and colors. For a computer, the equivalent of a "blank canvas" is pure random noise, which is computationally trivial to generate. If we wanted to teach a model to paint like a human, we would normally need a dataset containing thousands of intermediate sketches. Instead, Flow Matching creates this dataset **"on the fly"** during training by mathematically interpolating between pure noise and the final image.
- **Linear Interpolation (The Forward Process):** Instead of complex probabilistic noise schedules used in older diffusion models, the forward process is often framed as a simple straight-line interpolation between pure noise ($x_1$) and the target image ($x_0$): $x(t) = x_0(1-t) + x_1 t$. During training, a random timestep $t \in$ is uniformly sampled, creating an intermediate, partially noisy image at that exact point along the line.
- **Velocity Field Prediction:** The neural network (often a U-Net architecture) is trained to predict the **velocity field**—the derivative $\dot{x} = x_1 - x_0$, which represents the straight-line direction from the noise to the clean image. Crucially, the model is fed both the noisy image and the specific timestep $t$; informing the model of its current position along the trajectory makes it vastly easier for it to predict the correct denoising direction.
- **Inference via Euler's Method & The Danger of Drifting:** To generate a new, random image, we start with pure noise and use numerical integration (like Euler's Method) to iteratively follow the predicted velocity field.
    - **Step Size Matters:** We must divide the process into $N$ small steps (e.g., $N=10$, where $\Delta t = 0.1$). If we try to take a massive step (like $N=1$, attempting to generate the image in a single shot), the model's prediction error will be too high, causing it to fail completely.
    - **Drifting:** If the iterative steps are too large, the generation trajectory will "drift" away from the correct mathematical path, leading to accumulated errors and a completely corrupted or distorted final image. Small steps allow the model to continuously self-correct along the path.
- **Latent Flow Matching:** To make this process vastly more computationally efficient, modern implementations often perform flow matching in a **latent space** rather than raw pixel space. The target images are first compressed by an encoder into smaller latent representations. The entire interpolation, noise addition, and velocity prediction occur within this latent space, making the model faster to train and preventing issues where the model generates strange, non-reconstructable color artifacts.
